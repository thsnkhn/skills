# Default Voice

## Purpose

Use this as the default author voice for `linkedin-writer`.

It describes a specific, unnamed builder-operator voice. Do not add the author's name, byline, profile URL, employer history, or identifying browser metadata to a draft or explanation.

This profile distills a 365-day review of the author's posts ranked by impressions and engagement. It is a voice guide, not a claim about permanent LinkedIn algorithm rules. External benchmarks reinforced that strong posts can be a single sentence, a compact field note, or a long personal story. Substance and specificity matter more than one reusable structure.

## Voice Fingerprint

The author writes like someone pausing real work long enough to share what just happened.

The voice is:

- builder-to-builder, not expert-to-audience
- direct before polished
- specific before inspirational
- confident without performing certainty
- conversational without becoming careless
- playful without turning every post into a joke
- interested in products, teams, systems, founders, and the consequences of decisions
- comfortable ending on an observation instead of manufacturing a lesson

The post should feel like a useful field note, a sharp conclusion, or a real invitation. It should not feel like "content."

## Observed Cadence Anchors

These are short, anonymized fragments from posts that ranked strongly in the account sample. They are cadence evidence, not reusable hooks:

- `startups rarely fail for just one reason.`
- `if things fall apart when you step away for 7 days, that is not delegation. it is dependency.`
- `[brand] got the sparkle treatment.`
- `you can tell a lot about a team by how big their guidelines PDF is.`
- `crazy. [tool] is outperforming [tool] rn. built a native macOS downloader. two shots. already downloaded a 2GB torrent.`

They show several account-specific habits:

- The post often starts with the claim, result, or reaction immediately.
- Plain words and precise nouns do more work than polished metaphors.
- A strong thought may be one compact line; a raw build update may use fragments.
- Internet shorthand belongs only in genuinely immediate builder notes.
- The punchline is often an operational distinction, a concrete artifact, or an understated reaction.
- The ending usually stops on the thought instead of summarizing it for the audience.

Do not introduce tidy marketing rhythm that was not in the source. In particular:

| Too neat or generic | Closer to the observed voice |
|---|---|
| `push features, optimize, repeat.` | `it is now a weekend job where i push new features and optimize it.` |
| `we are thrilled by this game-changing milestone.` | `[project] crossed [verified number].` |
| `here are three lessons from the journey.` | State the single consequence or belief the work actually produced. |

Use the supplied facts and phrasing for the real draft. Do not recycle these fragments as templates.

## Favored Content Lanes

The account's strongest material consistently comes from these lanes.

### Real invitations and moments

Dinners, gatherings, hiring, feedback requests, releases, reservations, and other posts with a genuine reason to respond ranked strongly in this account sample. Treat that as an account-specific pattern, not proof of causality.

Foreground:

- what is happening
- who it is for
- why it is worth doing
- the concrete next action

Do not bury the invitation beneath a personal-brand lesson.

### Operator opinions with consequences

Strong opinion posts usually make one plain claim about founders, teams, clients, products, or operations, then show the consequence that made the claim worth saying.

Write from earned observation:

- what the author noticed
- what breaks or changes because of it
- what the author now believes

Avoid turning the observation into generic management advice.

### Raw builder notes

Experiments, fast builds, unexpected results, bugs, new tools, and work in progress suit an immediate, compressed delivery.

Keep the energy of discovery. Name the thing built, the constraint, and the result. A fragment or abrupt opening can work here when it reflects genuine surprise.

Do not clean a raw note until it sounds like a launch announcement.

### Milestones with proof

For downloads, stars, customer behavior, awards, press, shipping progress, and company wins, the voice is strongest when proof carries the emotion.

Lead with the result or the unlikely origin. Add enough context to show why it matters. Gratitude can be present, but it should be proportionate and specific.

Avoid adjective piles and ceremonial announcement language.

### Personal narratives

Use a longer narrative only when chronology, tension, scenes, or a change in belief are the substance.

Include details only the author could provide. Let the reader experience the turn rather than announcing a "lesson learned."

Do not stretch a small update into an origin story.

### Playful observations

Dry jokes, product-culture observations, understated celebrations, and lightly absurd comparisons are part of the voice. They work best as a treatment applied to real material, not as a separate content quota.

One good aside is usually enough.

## Opening Behavior

Open with the event, tension, result, or thesis itself.

Good opening behavior includes:

- stating what happened
- naming the experiment
- giving the result
- making the opinion
- locating the reader in a real moment
- beginning with an honest reaction when the reaction is the story

The first line does not need to be a "hook." It needs to be alive and true.

Prefer:

- a concrete noun over a broad theme
- the conclusion over throat-clearing
- a real surprise over manufactured suspense
- plain language over a dramatic claim

Avoid:

- "I'm thrilled to announce"
- "I never thought I would write this"
- "Here's the hard truth"
- "You won't believe what happened next"
- "X changed everything"
- a provocative line that the body does not earn
- a question asked only to manufacture curiosity

Do not withhold the interesting fact merely to create a reveal.

## Paragraph Rhythm

Use short, natural paragraphs organized by thought.

Typical rhythm:

- one to three sentences in a paragraph
- mixed sentence lengths
- a line break when the idea, time, or emotional beat changes
- an occasional one-line paragraph for emphasis
- fragments in raw builder notes when they preserve genuine momentum

A one-sentence post is valid when the thesis is complete. A long post is valid when the story earns the space.

Do not:

- isolate every sentence on its own line
- break one sentence into several theatrical fragments
- pad a concise observation into a seven-part framework
- compress a real story until all texture disappears
- use the same paragraph pattern in every draft

Read the post aloud. It should sound like one person talking, not a carousel presented as text.

## Proof Style

Proof should appear close to the claim it supports.

Useful proof includes:

- verified numbers and timeframes
- what was built, shipped, changed, or removed
- the product, tool, system, or artifact involved
- observed user behavior
- a supplied customer statement, quoted or accurately paraphrased
- a decision and its consequence
- a place, date, audience, and purpose for an invitation
- a publication, repository, demo, chart, or other inspectable artifact

Use the smallest amount of proof needed to make the point credible. Two meaningful numbers are better than a wall of vanity metrics.

Match proof to the mode:

- For an experiment, say what was tried and what happened.
- For a milestone, give the verified result and enough context to interpret it.
- For an opinion, give the operating consequence that produced the belief.
- For an invitation, give the practical details and the real reason to attend.
- For a narrative, use concrete scenes and chronological details.

Never invent or round up metrics, traction, customer reactions, timelines, quotes, or emotional stakes. If a claim such as "users are replacing an established tool with this" comes from supplied feedback, attribute or qualify it accurately. If it cannot be supported, remove it.

## Point of View

Facts make the post credible. The author's interpretation makes it worth reading.

After stating what happened, identify what the author finds:

- surprising
- funny
- uncomfortable
- encouraging
- strategically important
- different from the common assumption
- worth changing next

The point of view can be one sentence. It does not need to become a list of lessons.

Useful pivots sound like natural thought:

- the interesting part is not the headline result
- the small decision had a larger consequence
- the experiment stopped being an experiment
- the apparent success created more work
- the conventional explanation misses what actually happened

State uncertainty when it is real. Calm conviction and honest ambiguity can coexist.

## Humor and Emotional Register

Humor is dry, quick, and usually attached to a concrete detail.

Use:

- playful understatement
- one brief absurd exaggeration after the facts are clear
- self-awareness about the messiness of building
- an unexpected but accurate comparison
- occasional internet shorthand in a genuinely raw note

Avoid:

- a joke in every paragraph
- forced memes
- emoji decoration
- mocking customers, teammates, or people with less context
- inserting slang the author did not use or imply
- turning serious material into a bit
- manufacturing self-deprecation to appear relatable

Excitement should sound like excitement, not public-relations language. Prefer a plain "this is getting interesting" shape over "delighted," "honored," "revolutionary," or "game-changing."

Do not add profanity, emojis, or slang merely to simulate personality.

## Capitalization and Polish

Lowercase is a natural part of the voice, but it is not a costume.

Guidelines:

- Use mostly lowercase in conversational, opinion, milestone, and raw-builder posts when it feels natural.
- Lowercase first-person `i` is acceptable when the whole post is intentionally in that register.
- Use conventional capitalization when the source draft is more formal, the narrative benefits from it, or readability would improve.
- Do not switch randomly between lowercase and conventional sentence casing.
- Preserve official capitalization for products, companies, people, places, acronyms, and technical terms such as LinkedIn, GitHub, macOS, AI, and API.
- Preserve intentional punctuation and contractions.
- Correct accidental spelling errors, duplicated words, broken grammar, and malformed links.
- Do not manufacture typos to make the post appear casual.
- Do not over-polish informal phrasing that carries the author's cadence.

Casual should feel deliberate, not neglected.

## Strong Versus Formulaic

| Element | Formulaic version | Voice-aligned version |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Announces that an announcement is coming | States the event, result, or belief |
| Authority | Lectures from above | Shares an earned observation with peers |
| Proof | Uses broad claims and superlatives | Uses verified nouns, numbers, decisions, and consequences |
| Structure | Forces hook, story, lessons, and CTA | Uses only the parts the material needs |
| Paragraphs | Places every sentence on a separate line | Groups sentences by thought |
| Emotion | Says "thrilled," "honored," or "humbled" | Lets the event and a plain reaction carry the feeling |
| Insight | Extracts generic advice | Names the author's actual interpretation |
| Vulnerability | Adds a struggle-to-success arc | Includes uncertainty or tension only when real |
| Closing | Asks "What do you think?" by default | Stops on the completed thought or gives a real action |
| Hashtags | Adds a topical bundle | Uses none by default |
| First comment | Moves links for an assumed reach trick | Holds genuinely useful supporting material |
| Length | Targets a supposedly optimal template | Follows the substance |

A useful test: if the draft could be posted unchanged by any founder, agency owner, or product account, it is not specific enough yet.

## Content-mode Calibration

| Mode | Opening | Development | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw builder note | The experiment, surprise, or immediate result | A few concrete details about what was built and what happened | The honest reaction, new problem, or next step |
| Operator opinion | The conclusion or consequence | One or two observations that earned the belief | Stop when the implication is clear |
| Milestone with proof | The verified result or unlikely origin | Context, user behavior, and why the result matters | Understated excitement, gratitude, or what the milestone changed |
| Invitation or action | The occasion, place, audience, or purpose | Why this group or moment matters and the practical details | A direct, genuine CTA |
| Personal narrative | A specific scene, tension, or turning point | Chronology, choices, and details only the author can supply | The changed belief, unresolved tension, or present reality |

Choose the lightest mode that fits. Do not turn a builder note into a narrative or add a CTA to an opinion merely because those devices are available.

## Anonymized Shape Examples

These demonstrate cadence only. Every bracketed detail must come from supplied or verified source material.

### Raw builder note

this started as [small experiment].

[verified result] later, it has become [what it is now].

the interesting part is [actual interpretation].

### Operator opinion

[direct conclusion].

[concrete operating consequence that made it obvious].

### Milestone with proof

[product or project] crossed [verified milestone].

it began as [verified origin or constraint]. now [verified user behavior or consequence].

[plain reaction].

### Invitation

[place or occasion], [date].

we're bringing [specific group] together for [real purpose].

if that sounds like you, [direct action].

### Personal narrative

[specific scene or moment].

i believed [earlier belief]. then [real event or decision] changed the shape of it.

[what the author believes or sees now].

### Compact playful ending

[verified result].

not bad for [verified, modest origin].

Do not reuse these as fill-in-the-blank formulas. They show how the voice moves from source to proof to interpretation.

## First-comment Voice

A first comment should sound as direct as the post.

Use it when an article, app, repository, demo, source, chart, or technical note would interrupt the main thought. Write a complete, paste-ready sentence around the links.

Shape:

the full write-up is here, and [the product, demo, or source] is here:

[link]

[link]

Keep the central action in the post when the post is itself an invitation or request. Do not promise better reach from moving a link to the comments.

## Voice Audit Checklist

Before returning a draft, check:

- Is the post rooted in a real event, observation, artifact, decision, or belief?
- Could this author plausibly be the only person who wrote it?
- Does the opening state the actual thing instead of advertising that a story is coming?
- Are all numbers, dates, reactions, quotes, and outcomes supplied or verified?
- Is the strongest proof close to the claim it supports?
- Does the author's interpretation appear without becoming generic advice?
- Does the post speak to peers rather than lecture an audience?
- Are paragraphs grouped by thought instead of split for artificial drama?
- Does the length follow the material?
- Does lowercase feel natural and consistent?
- Are official names and technical terms capitalized correctly?
- Were accidental typos fixed without flattening the informal cadence?
- Is humor present only where the material supports it?
- Is the emotional language proportionate to what happened?
- Is the CTA tied to a real action?
- Is a first comment included only because it carries useful supporting material?
- Are hashtags, engagement bait, empty questions, and corporate announcement phrases absent?
- Has the draft stopped once the thought is complete?
- Does the output avoid the author's name, byline, profile URL, and identifying browser metadata?
